Two medical policies can print the same headline limit and behave completely differently the first time you need them. The limit is the least interesting number on the schedule. What matters is what happens when you or your family actually walk into a hospital.

Here is what we walk through with every client before we recommend a provider.

## 1. Start with the hospital, not the insurer

Ask which hospitals you realistically use. If your family goes to a facility in Nairobi’s private network and the plan only settles at public hospitals in its standard tier, the policy is wrong for you no matter how cheap it looks.

Every insurer publishes a hospital list by tier. Check it against your actual habits, including the facility near your rural home that you use at Christmas.

## 2. Understand what the outpatient limit really covers

The outpatient limit is where most families feel the policy week to week. Look at three things:

- Whether consultations are covered or only laboratory tests
- Whether prescribed medication is inside the limit or separate
- Whether there is a co-payment per visit, and how much it is

A KES 200,000 outpatient limit with a KES 1,000 co-payment per visit behaves very differently from a KES 100,000 limit with a KES 500 co-payment if you visit a clinic monthly.

## 3. Declare pre-existing conditions in writing

This is the single most common reason a Kenyan health claim is declined. If you have hypertension, asthma, diabetes, or a condition you are under review for, put it on the proposal form.

Declaring a condition does not automatically make it uninsurable. Providers handle it in one of three ways:

1. Cover immediately at standard rates
2. Cover after a waiting period, commonly six to twelve months
3. Cover with a premium loading

What is never available is cover for a condition you did not declare.

## 4. Check the maternity waiting period before you need it

If a pregnancy is planned, the maternity waiting period is nine to twelve months at most Kenyan providers. A policy taken out in January does not pay for a delivery in March, whatever the salesperson implied.

For employers, this matters for staff retention. Groups often negotiate the maternity waiting period down as part of the scheme terms.

## 5. Ask how chronic conditions are treated

HIV, cancer, diabetes, and hypertension are the conditions that make medical cover genuinely valuable. Ask explicitly:

- Is chronic medication on the outpatient limit or a separate chronic limit?
- Is oncology covered, and up to how much?
- Is dialysis covered, and is there an annual cap?

If the answer is vague, ask for it in writing before you pay. Our brokers get the chronic benefit schedule in writing for every client we place.

## 6. Look at the claims service, not the brochure

The test of a medical insurer is how fast it authorises an admission at 9pm on a Sunday. Ask two questions:

- Is there a 24 hour authorisation line, or do you wait for office hours?
- Does the insurer settle directly with the hospital, or reimburse you later?

Direct settlement through a smart card is standard for inpatient cover from all the major providers we place. If a plan only reimburses, you carry the bill until the claim is processed.
