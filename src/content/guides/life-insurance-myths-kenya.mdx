Life insurance is the product people avoid discussing and most regret not having. Most of the resistance comes down to seven myths we hear weekly in our Nairobi office.

## Myth 1: Life insurance is only for wealthy people

Term life is the cheapest cover sold in Kenya. A healthy 35 year old can often secure several million shillings of protection for less than the cost of a monthly family data bundle.

The people who need it most are the ones whose household income stops the moment they do: the single parent, the breadwinner with a mortgage, the farmer with school fees due in January.

## Myth 2: If I die, my family automatically gets the money

Not automatically. Two things decide what happens:

- **Nomination.** If you name beneficiaries on the policy, the insurer pays them directly and the money does not wait for the estate.
- **No nomination.** The benefit forms part of your estate, and the family waits for probate. In Kenya that can take a long time.

Check your nomination every time your circumstances change: marriage, a new child, a separation, or the death of a nominated person.

## Myth 3: The insurer will find a reason not to pay

Insurers decline claims for reasons that are usually avoidable, and they are almost always disclosure related:

- A health condition that was not declared on the proposal form
- A smoking habit recorded as non smoker
- A hazardous occupation or hobby that was left out

Two years of full and honest disclosure is what makes a claim at year ten completely straightforward.

## Myth 4: Premiums paid come back to me

That is true of endowment and whole life plans with a savings element, and it is not true of pure term cover. Term insurance buys protection, not savings. If you want the money back, you buy an education or endowment plan, and you pay more for it.

Neither is wrong. They do different jobs. What is wrong is buying a savings plan when what your family needs is a large sum assured today.

## Myth 5: Life insurance payouts are taxed heavily

Death benefits paid to nominated beneficiaries are generally not subject to income tax in Kenya. Speak to your tax adviser about your specific circumstances, but the tax fear is usually overstated.

## Myth 6: I have group life at work, so I am covered

Group life through an employer is valuable and worth having. Two caveats:

1. The cover usually ends when the employment ends, at the exact moment your income stops.
2. The sum assured is based on your salary, which may not reflect the debt and school fees your family would face.

Use group cover as a base and top it up with a personal policy you own.

## Myth 7: Suicide is never covered, so the policy is worthless

Every Kenyan life policy has a suicide clause, commonly excluding it during the first two years. After the waiting period most policies pay. It is a standard underwriting condition, not a trick, and it should not be the reason you avoid protecting your family.

## How much cover is enough

We calculate it, rather than guess:

| Component | Typical approach |
| --- | --- |
| Income replacement | 5 to 10 times annual income |
| Outstanding debts | The full mortgage or loan balance |
| Education | The estimated cost of schooling to university |
| Funeral and last expenses | A fixed amount, commonly KES 200,000 to 500,000 |
| Less existing cover | Group life, savings, and existing policies |

The total is your sum assured. Then we choose the product that delivers it at the lowest sustainable premium.
